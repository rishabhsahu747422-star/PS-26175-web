Autonomous Satellite-Based Oil Spill Detection and Lagrangian Kinematic Vessel Attribution in Coastal Corridors

A Multi-Modal Framework Integrating Synthetic Aperture Radar, Hydrodynamic Drift Simulation, and Automatic Identification System Telemetry

Abstract

Accidental and deliberate marine oil discharges constitute one of the most destructive threats to marine ecology and coastal economies. Conventional maritime aerial surveillance and patrol vessels suffer from limited spatiotemporal revisit rates and prohibitive operating costs. While modern Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) constellations (e.g., Sentinel-1) enable all-weather, day-and-night surface anomaly detection, establishing legal vessel attribution remains difficult due to slick advection caused by ocean currents and wind leeway during the temporal window between discharge and satellite overpass.

TRACEOIL introduces an automated attribution pipeline that: (1) isolates oil slicks in C-band SAR images through dual-polarization damping analysis, (2) extracts maritime vessel targets using Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) filters, (3) simulates reverse Lagrangian particle transport forced by ocean velocity fields (Copernicus Marine CMEMS) and surface wind vectors (ECMWF), and (4) correlates reconstructed discharge corridors against time-indexed AIS trajectory records. This article details the mathematical formulation, system scope, experimental design, and future research directions.

1. Research Motivation & Problem Statement

The world's commercial maritime fleet transports over 80% of international trade, including billions of barrels of crude petroleum and refined hydrocarbon fuels. While major catastrophic tanker groundings (such as the Exxon Valdez or Deepwater Horizon) capture global media attention, maritime environmental agencies estimate that the overwhelming majority of ocean hydrocarbon pollution stems from continuous, unauthorized operational discharges.

Vessels routinely perform illicit bilge-water purging, oily machinery sludge pumping, and cargo tank washing in international waters, predominantly during night passes to prevent optical detection. Under the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships (MARPOL 73/78, Annex I), oily bilge water discharges exceeding 15 parts per million (ppm) within designated special areas are strictly criminalized. However, practical enforcement remains crippled by three severe bottlenecks:

1. Spatiotemporal Gap

Maritime patrol aircraft can inspect only a minuscule fraction of oceanic exclusive economic zones (EEZs) on any single sortie.

2. Physical Advection

A slick moves continuously due to surface currents and wind drift. By the time it is imaged, the guilty ship has traveled dozens of nautical miles away.

3. Evidentiary Rigor

Maritime courts require irrefutable spatiotemporal correlation between the slick’s genesis and the ship’s kinematic profile.

2. Remote Sensing Physics: SAR Surface Damping

Spaceborne Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is the primary remote sensing tool for marine oil spill observation because it emits microwave radiation (C-band ~5.4 GHz on Sentinel-1) that penetrates cloud cover and functions independently of solar illumination.

Under moderate wind conditions (between 3 m/s and 12 m/s), radar backscatter over open oceans is dominated by Bragg scattering, where microwave pulses resonate with wind-generated capillary and short gravity waves (wavelengths \(\lambda_B = \lambda / (2 \sin \theta)\)). When mineral oil or hydrocarbons form an insoluble surface film, the surface tension of water changes abruptly, suppressing capillary wave generation.

However, SAR detection is complicated by "lookalikes" — natural biological films, grease ice, low wind calms, rain cells, and internal waves that also produce dark patches.

3. Proposed Research Approach

TRACEOIL unifies remote sensing computer vision with hydrodynamic backward trajectory physics and maritime AIS intelligence. The system operates across four coordinated stages:

  1. Multispectral & SAR Radiometric Preprocessing: Ingestion of Sentinel-1 Level-1 GRD products, applying precise orbit files, thermal noise removal, calibration to backscatter coefficient \(\sigma^0\), Refined Lee speckle filtering, and land masking via high-resolution GSHHG shorelines.
  2. Adaptive Semantic Slick Segmentation: Employing adaptive spatial thresholding and deep neural architectures to separate mineral slicks from lookalikes using contextual textural descriptors (contrast, homogeneity, angular second moment).
  3. Lagrangian Reverse-in-Time Drift Modeling: Backtracking observed slick centroids toward their probable discharge origin points using Navier-Stokes surface current vectors from Copernicus Marine Environment Monitoring Service (CMEMS) and 10-meter wind fields from ECMWF.
  4. AIS Kinematic Spatiotemporal Corroboration: Reconstructing the historical voyages of all commercial vessels within the attribution bounding box, interpolating intermittent AIS reports, and calculating attribution probability vectors based on proximity, heading, and speed anomalies.

4. Mathematical Drift & Attribution Formulation

The physical displacement of an oil parcel floating on the ocean surface is governed by the vector sum of ambient sea surface current velocity, wind leeway advection, and horizontal turbulent diffusion:

\(\frac{d\vec{x}}{dt} = \vec{u}_{\text{current}}(\vec{x}, t) + \gamma \cdot \vec{u}_{\text{wind}}(\vec{x}, t) + \vec{u}_{\text{diffusion}}(\vec{x}, t)\)

Where:

  • \(\vec{u}_{\text{current}}\) is the Eulerian surface current velocity vector \((u, v)\) provided at hourly intervals by CMEMS numerical ocean models.
  • \(\vec{u}_{\text{wind}}\) is the 10-meter wind velocity vector from ECMWF atmospheric reanalysis.
  • \(\gamma\) is the empirical wind leeway factor, typically parameterized as \(\gamma \in [0.030, 0.035]\) (3.0% to 3.5% of wind speed) with a deflection angle \(\theta_{\text{defl}}\) of \(0^\circ\) to \(15^\circ\) to the right in the Northern Hemisphere due to Coriolis forcing.
  • \(\vec{u}_{\text{diffusion}}\) represents stochastic Brownian turbulent spreading parameterized by horizontal diffusivity \(K_h\).

To trace a spill back in time from satellite observation time \(T_{\text{sat}}\) to an earlier candidate discharge time \(t\), the trajectory is integrated backward using a fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) numerical scheme:

\(\vec{x}(t - \Delta t) = \vec{x}(t) - \int_{t-\Delta t}^{t} \vec{V}_{\text{drift}}(\vec{x}(\tau), \tau) \, d\tau\)

6. Known Limitations & Future Directions

Scientific & Operational Boundaries

Wind Speed Window: SAR capillary damping is only reliable between 3.0 m/s and 12.0 m/s. At wind speeds below 2.5 m/s, the sea produces zero Bragg scattering, creating widespread natural dark zones. At wind speeds exceeding 14 m/s, vigorous wave breaking rapidly emulsifies the oil and disrupts the surface layer.

AIS Dark Target Behavior: Malicious actors intentionally disable their transponders before discharge. In such scenarios, attribution must rely on secondary indicators such as vessel wake geometry and forward trajectory extrapolation.

Future Research Roadmap

Future iterations of TRACEOIL will investigate:

  • Incorporation of hyperspectral satellite constellations (e.g., EnMAP, PRISMA) for oil chemical fingerprinting and slick thickness estimation.
  • Coupling deep physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) with ocean drift models to improve turbulence parameterization near coastal surf zones.
  • Real-time automated webhook integration with national maritime coast guard command centers.
